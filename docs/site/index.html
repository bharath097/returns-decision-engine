Recovery Lab

Models, decisions and a pipeline for a business that sells returned goods: what each item will recover, how to list it, where labor pays, what a truckload is worth, and what each decision is worth in dollars.

All data is simulated with known ground truth, so each method can be checked against the truth it tries to recover. Read the numbers as a test of method, not as facts about any real company.
What this is

A decision system for a marketplace that sells returned and overstock goods

The business buys returns and overstock by the truckload, opens them, matches each item to a catalog entry, photographs and grades it, and sells it by auction or at a fixed price. The measure that matters is recovery: what an item sold for divided by what it would have cost new, with unsold items counted as zero.

This project builds the analytics layer for that business. It predicts what each item will recover, decides how to list it, where labor is worth spending, and which truckloads to bid on. It answers report requests from governed definitions. And it prices every one of those decisions in dollars per month, with an honest statement of how well each number is known.

The problem

Five things make this hard

A zero is a zero. An unsold auction item recovers nothing, so averages over sold items overstate how well the business is doing. Two channels run on different clocks: an auction ends in two days, a fixed price has no end, so they cannot be compared on a shared window. Contents are unknown. A truckload can hold toys and phones together, with items from $5 to thousands of dollars. Labor is the main variable cost, so every extra minute of handling has to earn its place. Definitions drift. Without governed definitions, "sales last week" means different things to different people, and to an assistant answering for them.

How it works

One pipeline, eleven steps, one gate at the front

Raw files go into a SQL warehouse. A set of data tests runs on it, and if any error-level test fails, the run stops: nothing downstream is trusted on bad data. After that each stage answers one question, hands what it learned to the next, and writes its tables to outputs/tables. The last stages combine everything into per-item decisions and a dollar value for each lever, then monitor the models and answer report requests.

What it found

Six findings, then the evidence for each

  1. Five items cannot settle the fixed-price question; 31,420 can. Three of five selling is consistent with a true sale rate anywhere from 23% to 88%, and the break-even is 33%. At scale the strategy lowers net value by 0.013 per retail dollar (95% interval -0.020 to -0.005) and takes 14.1 days instead of 4.1.
  2. The recovery model is better than the lookup it would replace. Average miss 0.169 against 0.187, and total predicted dollars within 2.5% of actual against +12.6%. On its own it is worth about $1,530 a month. The value comes from the decisions it feeds.
  3. The best channel depends on the category. Fixed price wins for Large appliances +0.070. Auction wins for 5 categories (Laptops current -0.056, Smartphones -0.052, Gaming -0.052, Beauty health -0.033, Toys games -0.029). The other 9 cannot be told apart yet. Item-level routing gains $11,958 a month on paper, but its interval ($3,315 to $20,343) includes zero.
  4. A price that steps down with age beats any single held price by 0.008 per retail dollar inside the tested range ($2,752 a month). More comes from outside the range, which is untested.
  5. Labor should follow value. Extra photos pay from the 100-250 band up and lose money below $100. Auditing the 5% of catalog matches most likely to be wrong catches 75% of the mis-stated retail dollars.
  6. Truckload value can be predicted before opening (correlation 0.98). A bid ceiling doubles profit per dollar spent (0.16 to 0.36) but whether total profit rises depends on how often sellers accept a counter-offer, which this data cannot tell us.

Each finding below is laid out the same way: the question, how it was looked at and whether ML was used, what the data shows, and what to do about it.

Judging a pilot

Five items, three sold at +20%, two still at zero: does the strategy work?

The question. Five items were listed at the expected auction price plus 20%. Three sold, 20% above expectation. The other two are at zero, and in an auction a zero is a zero. Recovery on sold items and recovery on all items tell different stories. Which one should decide whether the strategy works?

How I looked at it

Count every item listed, including the zeros. Value of fixed-price-first = chance it sells x (1.2 x expected price) + chance it does not x (expected price minus the cost of waiting, then it goes to auction). That gives a break-even sale probability. Then check whether the evidence can tell us which side of it we are on, first with five items, then with the randomized pilot in the warehouse.

Where ML is used

Wilson interval for the five items (statistics, no ML). At scale: randomized assignment, then a cross-fitted uplift model (see Choosing a channel) to ask which items benefit.

What the data shows

With a wait cost of 10% of the item's value, fixed-price-first beats auction when more than 33% of items sell. Three of five is 60%, but five items allow a true rate anywhere from 23% to 88%, which spans the break-even. The honest answer from five items is "not enough evidence".

The warehouse has the same experiment at scale: 31,420 items, randomly assigned. At +20%, 60% sell at the fixed price, well above the 33% break-even, yet the whole strategy still comes out 0.011 per retail dollar behind auction and takes 14 days. The arithmetic and the pilot disagree. The most likely reason is that waiting costs more than the 10% I assumed, which is why the break-even rule is a screen and not a verdict.

The three bars on the right are the same items scored three ways. Counting only items that sold, fixed price looks better (0.49 at +30% against 0.44). Counting zeros in the first channel, it looks much worse. Valuing the whole strategy, including what happens after rollover, it is close to or below auction. Only the third view is a fair comparison.

So what. Judge a channel on net value per item listed, with zeros counted and the fallback included. To detect a difference of 0.02 per retail dollar you need about 4,290 items per arm; for 0.01, 17,159. A five-item pilot can only be an anecdote, and the report should say so before anyone acts on it.

Predicting recovery

How much will an item recover, before it is listed?

The question. Recovery is what an item sold for divided by its retail value, with unsold items counted as zero. A model that predicts it from history and product features is the base for every later decision.

How I looked at it

Recovery has a spike at zero, so one regression on a blended average hides the problem. I model it in two parts: the chance the item sells, and the recovery given that it sells. The product is calibrated with isotonic regression, and conformal intervals say how wide the miss can be. Evaluation uses a time split, not a random one, because the model will always be used on the future.

Where ML is used

Yes. Two LightGBM models (a classifier and a regressor) with isotonic calibration and split-conformal intervals. Compared with the incumbent category x condition lookup, a category average and a naive sold-only model.

What the data shows

On items listed after the training window the two-part model misses by 0.169 on average, against 0.187 for the lookup. Its predicted total dollars are -2.5% off; the lookup and the naive model are both about +12.6% too high, because they ignore the zeros. Probability-of-sale AUC is 0.821; the 80% intervals cover 78.9% of outcomes.

What this is worth depends on what it is used for. As the anchor for a fixed-price listing with no premium, it adds $1.23 per item (95% interval $0.92 to $1.68), about $1,530 a month. Perfect item-level knowledge would add $3.37, so there is still room. At a +20% premium the same improvement is worth -$0.04 per item, effectively nothing, because the premium decision dominates the anchor.

So what. A more accurate number is not a result. It becomes one when the decision using it (price, channel, markdown) is also right, which is why the channel and markdown questions follow, and why the final ladder reports dollars and not error.

Evidence: recovery_metrics, recovery_calibration, recovery_grouped_cv, interval_coverage_by_category, pricing_anchor_impact

Choosing a channel

Auction or fixed price: for which items, and how sure are we?

The question. Some items could be sold at a fixed price instead of by auction. How do we know whether that is better, and for which items?

How I looked at it

A randomized pilot (items assigned by coin flip to auction or fixed price at +10, +20 or +30%), compared on net value per retail dollar so that big and small items are weighted fairly. Effects by category come with 95% intervals. A learned policy asks which individual items would do better in fixed price; its value is estimated with inverse-probability weights, and its interval from a bootstrap across lots.

Where ML is used

Yes. A T-learner uplift model (one LightGBM per arm, cross-fitted by lot so no item is scored by a model that saw its lot). The category forest plot is plain statistics.

What the data shows

On average, fixed-price-first is worse: -0.013 per retail dollar (interval -0.020 to -0.005). The average hides a split. Fixed price clearly wins for Large appliances +0.070. Auction clearly wins for Laptops current -0.056, Smartphones -0.052, Gaming -0.052, Beauty health -0.033, Toys games -0.029. The remaining 9 categories are inside the noise at this sample size.

Choosing the channel item by item with the uplift model earns $89.74 per item against $85.38 for always-auction, a gain of $11,958 per month at current volume, but the bootstrap interval is $3,315 to $20,343. The point estimate is positive and the evidence is not strong enough to promise it.

So what. Start with a category rule (fixed price for the categories that clearly benefit, auction for the ones that clearly do not), keep a randomized holdback, and let the item-level model earn its place as the holdback grows.

Evidence: pilot_ate, pilot_ate_by_category, policy_comparison, uplift_calibration, pilot_balance

Waiting and markdown

A fixed price has no countdown: how long to wait, and at what price?

The question. A fixed-price item has no two-day clock like an auction. Comparing the two on a shared time window does not work, so what should replace the window?

How I looked at it

Compare the two channels by net return per day, not by a shared deadline. For each category, fit how fast items sell at each price and age (a discrete-time hazard model). Hold one more day only if the chance of selling times the gain exceeds the cost of waiting plus the value that decays. Solving that backward over a ladder of prices gives a markdown schedule, and a rollover to auction when the price falls below what auction would pay.

Where ML is used

Partly. Per-category complementary log-log hazard models, with the price effect shrunk toward the pooled value (empirical Bayes), feed a dynamic program. The schedule is arithmetic on top of the models.

What the data shows

Inside the price range the pilot actually tested (+10% to +30%), a step-down schedule earns 0.303 net value per retail dollar against 0.295 for the best single held price, a gain of $2,752 a month. Letting the ladder go below the tested range raises the model's value to 0.322, but that is extrapolation, and I report it separately for that reason.

Fast categories do not need the ladder to be long; slow ones do. The schedules are different for each category (left chart) because the sale curve flattens at different speeds, which matches the intuition that a popular phone sells in days and an older laptop does not.

So what. Run a randomized markdown test next, with prices below +10%, before treating the extrapolated gain as real. The tested-range gain is the number to plan on.

Evidence: waiting_policy_results, waiting_schedules, waiting_hazard_params, hazard_calibration, sale_curves_by_premium

Labor

Where is extra labor worth its cost?

The question. Handling an item costs labor, and US warehouse labor is expensive. An extra minute of photos may be worth it on a thousand-dollar item and not on a five-dollar one. Where is the line?

How I looked at it

A randomized experiment gave some items extra photos. The lift in sale price is small and noisy item by item, so I pool it across categories (shrinkage) and compare the benefit with the labor minutes. Matching an item to the right catalog entry is the other labor step; a wrong match mis-states retail value, which is the denominator of every recovery number. A classifier ranks items by how likely the match is wrong, so audits go where the dollars are.

Where ML is used

Photos: no, randomized experiment and break-even arithmetic. Match audit: yes, a classifier trained on audited items ranks the queue.

What the data shows

Extra photos lift the sale price by about 4%. After labor, that pays from the 100-250 retail band up; below $100 each item loses a little. Doing it for everyone would cost about $707 a month on cheap items; doing it only where it pays earns about $10,235 a month.

Sending the 5% of items most likely to have a wrong match to an auditor catches 75% of the dollars of mis-stated retail value (35% of the wrong matches), against 5% by random review. That protects the recovery model more than it adds profit by itself, which is why it is not on the profit ladder.

So what. Write the photo rule in one line the floor can follow ('three more photos on anything over $100'), and put the match audit upstream of every other model.

Evidence: photo_rule_by_band, photo_effects_by_category, min_item_value, match_review_gain

Truckloads

What is a truckload worth before anyone opens it?

The question. Truckloads arrive with unknown contents: toys and phones together, items from $5 to thousands of dollars. What should the business pay before opening one?

How I looked at it

Predict a truckload's net value from its manifest and source history, with a measured error for each source. Score sources on profit, quality, reliability, manifest accuracy and speed, with raw averages pulled toward the pooled mean when a source has few lots. A bid ceiling is the expected value, less freight and a buffer for uncertainty, divided by one plus the margin I want. I also price the value of opening a few items before bidding.

Where ML is used

Yes. A lot-value model whose predictions are made out-of-fold, plus per-source error. The scorecard is statistics (empirical Bayes); the ceiling is arithmetic on the model's output.

What the data shows

Predicted lot value tracks actual with a correlation of 0.98 on lots the model never saw. A bid ceiling set from it has a clear effect on efficiency: buying only lots listed under the ceiling returns 0.36 of profit per dollar spent, against 0.16 for buying everything. It also buys only 262 of 800 lots and earns less total profit.

The middle option is to counter-offer at the ceiling when a lot is over it. Whether that raises total profit depends entirely on how often the seller says yes. At 50% acceptance it adds about $7,520 a month; at 25% it loses $2,220. This data has no acceptance history, so the right figure to plan on is a range, and the first job is to start logging counter-offers. Opening 20 items from a typical lot costs about $37 in labor and is worth about $95 in better bids.

So what. Use the ceiling to decide where to negotiate and where to walk away, and log every counter-offer and response. That log turns the assumption-dependent bar on the ladder into a measured one.

Evidence: lot_oof_predictions, source_scorecard, bid_backtest, bid_sweep, sampling_value

Report requests

A metric layer that asks before it guesses

The question. People ask an assistant for reports, which only works if the data is documented. When someone asks for sales by category last week, the assistant must know whether that means gross or net sales, what a category is and where the data lives, or ask.

How I looked at it

Every metric is defined once in a file with the same seven fields: name, definition, calculation, source table, owner, refresh and intended use. A request is parsed for metric, period and breakdown. If a word is ambiguous (sales, revenue) the layer asks one clarifying question; if it is documented (recovery means all items, zeros counted) it answers and states the assumption. Questions it cannot map are logged.

Where ML is used

No model. Deterministic rules over governed definitions, so the answer is reproducible. The same file is exported as a prompt for an assistant to use.

What the data shows

Examples from the test set below. 'What were sales last week?' gets a question back; 'gross sales last week by category' gets an answer with the week boundaries and the date field it used. An unknown request lists the metrics that do exist, so the failure teaches the asker something.

Putting it together

One engine runs every model on an item, one ladder prices every lever

The question. A better prediction is only worth something if it changes a decision. What is each improvement worth in dollars per month, and how sure are we?

How I looked at it

Run every model on an item at intake and write down the single action it implies: predicted recovery with a range, channel, price plan, photos, audit. Then price each lever per month at current volume, say how that number was obtained, and refuse to add levers that overlap.

Where ML is used

This is where the models connect: the recovery model sets the anchor, the uplift model picks the channel, the hazard models set the schedule, the match model decides who is audited, the lot model sets the bid.

What the data shows

Twelve held-out items from twelve categories, each processed by every model. The actual outcome falls inside the 80% range in 6 of 12 rows, and the ranges are wide, which is the honest picture: one item is hard to predict, a thousand are not.

After launch

Monitoring: how the system notices it is going stale

The question. A model that was right at launch goes stale as the business changes. How does the system notice, and what does it do about it?

How I looked at it

Track error by month against the incumbent, and track input drift with the population stability index. A retrain rule fires when drift is high or error degrades. State what the data cannot support, and list the first work in order.

Where ML is used

Monitoring is statistics on the model's inputs and errors. The retrain rule is a documented threshold, not a model.

What the data shows

Retraining is recommended right now. Inputs that moved most: listing_moy, source_type, source_id, acquisition_rate. That is the June 2026 expansion (a new source type) plus seasonality. The two-part model beats the incumbent in every month, by 0.036 to 0.046. The sale-timing model over-predicts sales after the expansion, which a rolling recalibration only partly fixes.

What this data cannot say. It is simulated, so every number is a demonstration of method, not a claim about any real business. Within it, the markdown gain outside the tested range, the item-level routing gain, and the counter-offer gain are not established. Category effects with intervals that include zero are not effects.

What runs after launch

The pipeline runs weekly. Each run rebuilds the warehouse, stops if a data test fails, retrains and re-scores everything, and appends one line to outputs/run_history.csv with the headline numbers and fingerprints of the data and configuration that produced them. A change in those numbers can always be traced to a change in the data, the configuration or the code.

So what. A model is not finished when it is trained. It is finished when something is watching it, and when the numbers it produces can be traced to the data that made them.

Evidence: monthly_performance, drift_psi, data_quality_tests, run_history.csv, run_manifest.json

Why trust it

How the project is built to be checked

The data is simulated with known truth. The simulator knows each item's real value, each truckload's real contents and each source's real quality, so every method can be graded against the answer it is trying to recover. It also injects the defects real data has: duplicate rows, wrong catalog matches, and a mid-period expansion that shifts the mix.

A quality gate guards the front. 15 data tests run on the warehouse (13 pass, 2 warn, 0 fail). Any error-level failure stops the pipeline. 32 code tests cover the simulator, warehouse, models, decisions, metric layer and the pipeline's own gate.

Runs are reproducible and traceable. Two runs on the same data and configuration give the same numbers. Every run writes run_manifest.json (data fingerprint ad25eede08a37ed8, configuration fingerprint 5dff2a36aafb9089, rows per table, seconds and tables per stage, models saved) and appends to run_history.csv. Trained models are saved as artifacts and described in a model card.

Limits

What it cannot tell you

All numbers come from simulated data, so they demonstrate a method and say nothing about any real business. Within that: the markdown gain below the tested price range, the item-level routing gain and the counter-offer gain are not established. A category effect whose interval includes zero is not an effect. Levers overlap, so their dollar values must not be added. Anything the data cannot show, such as how often a seller accepts a counter-offer, is flagged as an assumption in the section where it matters.
